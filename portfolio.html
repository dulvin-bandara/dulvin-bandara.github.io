Project 01

Accumulator Cooling — Ducting Design and CFD

Monash Motorsport · Senior Aerodynamics Engineer, Head of Cooling subsection · Ansys Fluent 25R1, PyFluent, M3 MASSIVE HPC · 2026

Why are we cooling?

The M26 accumulator for the 2026 Monash Motorsport car is a 120s1p, 6.38 kWh air-cooled pack. The problem is that the cooling configuration presented in January has poor flow management and no consideration of airflow at the vehicle level. My solution for the project aims to develop a more vehicle-flow-focused cooling system, reducing the overall accumulator temperatures without impacting the overall vehicle-flow and integration.

Cooling exists so the pack is able to run a full endurance duty cycle at a reduced capacity. PACSIM simulations showed a 4.8 kWh pack is able to match the endurance performance of a 9.4kg heavier uncooled pack. Following 2026 points sensitivities, it was estimated that a reduction in mass by 9.4 kg provided a competition points gain of 20.57, a high sensitivity parameter vital for overall car performance.

Developing the Ducting

Airflow has to be brought into the accumulator from somewhere, and through preliminary simulations, the ducting was competing for air volume from the side diffuser and undertray, which are aerodynamically very costly parts. The side diffuser CLA dropped from 1.369 to 0.963, and the undertray dropped from 0.980 to 0.686, compared with a regular sealed duct reference case.

Following more targeted design iterations, a “sidepod” esque external duct was investigated, inspired by the ground-effect era Formula 1 cars. This proved to be a vital stepping stone in solving the CLA drop, pushing it to an overall 5.174, from a costly 4.406 in the sealed duct case. The aerodynamic balance of the car also shifted from 51.1% to 50%, an overall benefit there as well. The sidepod also contains angled louvres towards the bottom, providing a water ingress concern solution.

Investigating the external ducts, studies were done on the positioning of the inlet hole, inlet hole and other performance enhancers. Investigating the overall inlet positioning in the x direction was a key factor in maximising the flow into and through the accumulator.

Another concern, brought up during design reviews was water ingress into the accumulator. To account for this 2 options were looked into, a waterproof mesh and louvres. Physically speaking if there was no airflow impact the waterproof mesh was the most viable option. Ordering a waterproof mesh and conducting some tests in an wind-tunnel esque set-up below showed to block approximately 85-90% of the airflow, proving the mesh to be infeasible.

The louvres were looked at from then onwards at the bottom of the sidepod design. Taking 3 louvres with dimensions []. Provided the best water pathway per preliminary testing. During investigation of the louvres, a sweep of various angles with reference to the original positioning was conducted, unfortunately during the sweep the 12 degree angled louvre failed during the mesh process. However the following results were gathered. Noting the results as the louvre angle increased the mass flow into the monocoque did decrease, however very minimally. On the opposite end the CLA did increase through air leaking onto the side diffuser and consequently into the undertray. Taking both factors into consideration the CLA benefit overrode the overall mass flow deficit due to it being so small and thus a louvre angle of 15 degrees.

Vortex generators were a final late addition onto the external ducts, for 2 reasons generating high energy vortices to reduce air flow separation in the ducts, since water being more dense than air pushing the air upwards while the water would leak down through the louvres with gravity.

In parallel with these external ducts, internal ducting from the monocoque to the accumulator and from the accumulator outletting through the rear bulkhead were developed. In tandem with the external ducts, the key indicators of a good design were maximising the mass flow of air entering the accumulator and the overall uniformity of the velocity. Why? The mass flow is a key indicator of the volume of air being pushed through; the higher the mass flow, the greater the heat rejection available, bounded by what the fan can deliver. The uniformity is a scale from 0-1 showing how the velocity varies over a surface, with a value of 1 indicating perfectly uniform velocity over a surface, with lower values increasing the variation in velocity, using an area-weighted uniformity. By comparing these 2 metrics from the monocoque internal hole to the entrance of the accumulator, we were able to identify how the changes in ducting design impacted the hypothesised cooling performance.

The design of the internal ducting had a reverse ducting system due to the entrance of the accumulator being further forward than the monocoque internal wall. To account for any energy losses in the air that might occur, an investigation into turning vanes was conducted, improving the overall airflow uniformity and mass flow of the air entering the accumulator.

CFD Simulation Set-Up

The CFD simulation was conducted using a half-car symmetry model, taking into account ground effect and rotating wheels. The cooling pipeline, in particular, treated the accumulator as a container with segments split into the cell tabs and overall cells. The monocoque was hollowed out to account for any internal flow that may occur while unlikely in a sealed ducting system. A pressure-jump fan boundary condition was applied to the radiator and rear bulkhead fans, with a realizable k-epsilon with enhanced wall treatment turbulence model being used. Convergence was driven via report monitors on CLA & CDA, using PRESTO! for pressure interpolation, pseudo-transient continuation, and a velocity inlet specified by 5% intensity and a viscosity ratio of 10.

The final mesh contained 35.5M cells, with a skewness of 5.54E-11 and an orthogonal quality of 0.94. During the course of the campaign, multiple mesh debugs were taken into consideration through various self-intersection methods when defining fans and ensuring interior zones were properly defined to solve the fans.

Summary of Project

Over the course of the project, 94 design runs were completed, averaging approximately 5 hours per run. The mass flow rate through the accumulator was recorded at 0.021 kg/s, a massive improvement over the previously presented 0.008 kg/s, and a velocity uniformity of 0.943. Furthermore, the CLA had increased by 0.768, with the centre of pressure of the car moving from 48.8% to 50% from initial cooling runs. Current work focuses on manufacturing the ducts and finalising the thermal values, a direct measure of the work.

Project 02

Production CFD pipeline — PyFluent on HPC - With Cooling Integration

Monash Motorsport · Ansys Fluent 25R1, PyFluent, SLURM, M3 MASSIVE cluster · 2026

To conduct project 1 a workflow containing the fans, thermal simulation, and accumulator ducting was required to conduct design iterations. From the current TUI workflow this did not exist, thus with any cooling decisions required it was physically impossible to quantify any gain or losses predicted through both a thermal perspective and the overall impact to the car. This project focused on making that possible and developing an automated integrated cooling CFD workflow, to quantify any cooling decisions and conduct design iterations.

Prelim Investigations

Since the fan boundary condition was an unknown region being explored for the first time, understanding the basics on how Fluent handles it was vital. Through testing via various isolated block fan models, to define a fan boundary condition applied onto a face there had to be cells modelled in front and behind that face. While in theory this seems fairly simple, in practice proved to be a challenge. Our current pipeline once the geometry was imported in to complete the surface mesh a “wrap” is conducted placing an infinite large blanket over the geometry at a set drapability index to define the edges of the geometry. A set material point which is an arbitrary point somewhere in the domain grows volume cells out of it, stopping as it hits the previously wrapped surface. The issue this creates is, as it hits a solid such as a fan cylinder it won’t define any volume cells behind the fan face and thus unable to define the fan boundary condition.

Fan Definition Fixes

The first fix for this is to model the fan face as an infinitely thin circle, in theory this seemed perfect, with something being infinitely thin this should allow cells to propagate in front of and behind the fan face thus allowing the fan boundary condition to be placed onto it. Theory was right it did but the same positive that allowed this solution to be explored was unfortunately its downfall as well. By making the surface infinitely thin the cells were defined in front and behind the surface but also caused those cells to overlap and intersect each other. Tetrahedral initialization failed followed by segmentation failure, it was an obvious topological CAD error and not worth exploring more.

The second fix looked at modelling the fan as a 3D solid with the obvious choice being a cylinder. For convention sake the face facing the front of the car is the fan face, with the rearward face defined as the fan outlet. Following this new geometry the proposed fix was to utilise material points. How? As mentioned prior a material point grows volume cells out of the specified location stopping once hitting a wall specified by the wrap. Following this logic, if volume cells are emanating out from an arbitrary point until it hits the fan cylinder exterior wall, defining an extra material point inside the fan cylinder should develop the cells required inside the fan to define the fan boundary condition. Therefore the process was getting the coordinate in the centre of the fan cylinder, either allowing Fluent to convert the coordinates via the applied vehicle attitudes, and then defining the material points. Through initial trials, the material points for some fans were defining whereas some were not, investigating this between coordinates that Fluent recorded after the attitude definitions and the coordinates Fluent should be recording via hand calcs with the deviations summarised in Table 1. In some fans we can note some significant deviations in the coordinates, which is hypothesised to be a reason why the material points weren’t conducting the required duty. Following this fixes were tested primarily involving making the mesh finer around the specified region and increasing the drapeability of the wrap, however to no avail. Unfortunately this fix did not end up working.

Fix 3 took things a step back and looked at the problem more simply, instead of taking the current CFD architecture as given and adding to it, I looked at ways to adjust the architecture to my means. What did that mean? The thought was instead of using 1 wrap over the geometry, but to use 2 wraps! The first wrap included all geometry as normal, with the second wrap including all geometry except for the fans. The first wrap defined the edges of the fan cylinder defining the exterior and ensuring the fan geometry is noted, with the second wrap setting up the interior of the fans were taken note of. Once the volume mesh grows out the interior of the fan is defined too and provides a fix to the issue faced!

From this fix, the fan boundary conditions were able to be defined as required allowing the solving sequence to proceed and quantifiable look at mass flow values, and the overall impact to the car flow cooling design runs had.

Workflow Migration to PyFluent

Towards the middle of this year, the team began a migration of the existing TUI workflow to an improved PyFluent workflow. What positives did this provide? The PyFluent workflow was hosted via GitHub allowing for greater single version-controlled files allowing to safely save, review and merge any code changes. Furthermore, PyFluent allowed for individual CAD imports with individually defined scoped sizing and BOI’s where previously a group import was done and general scoped sizing’s and BOI’s were defined. PyFluent also posed no significant impact to the SLURM files with the minor addition of a virtual environment (VENV) requirement to host the required PyFluent modules.

My role posed implementing the current proven working TUI workflow into the PyFluent workflow being built. This proved to be fairly simple and useful allowing to optimise the mesh further in specific regions such as the finer region of the accumulator and not over-optimising unrequired regions. Once everything merged I was able to note a 54% reduction in runtime, heavily benefiting the efficiency of design runs. A key addition I added in, was unit tests specifically tailored to the errors I personally had faced when making code changes and noticing late on into a run losing all that progress. A total of 22 unit tests were created with all running in around 0.22 seconds saving key moments of time late on into a run. Over 2026 a total of 238 cases were completed and 35.5M cells over a full car case.

Post-Processing and Visualisation

At the end of the day all of this work is useless if we aren’t able to both visualise and quantify impacts from design runs; post-processing the data gained from these simulations are key. The current pipeline contained a working model of Paraview able to generate video contour sweeps for CpT, and other important parameters. What is that? CpT is the coefficient of total pressure which basically acts as a measure of the energy in the air acting as the sum of the dynamic and static pressure which more simply are the kinetic and potential energies of the air respectively; CpT in particular allows to manipulate the flow, with high CpT extremely useful for guiding duct flow and generating downforce. The script was manipulated to produce sweeps including the hollow monocoque and ducting system key for the design runs.

Further, there were key sections of interest that I was keen in getting a closer view on, in particular the entrance zone to the monocoque and the entrance to the accumulator. In my eyes these 2 particular sections dictated the overall cooling of the monocoque, the monocoque inlet flow was directly related to the geometry of the external duct and impacted the flow entering into the accumulator as setting a limit on what can be entered in. The accumulator inlet worked similarly in being the final stop of what the air would look like before beginning to cool the cells. In essence what I aimed to do was maximise the monocoque inlet flow as much as possible and limit any losses between the monocoque inlet and accumulator inlet, trying to keep the contours the same. To develop these contours I first defined planes via attitude changed CAD coordinates in Fluent, which exported key parameters into .csv’s, these csv’s then pass through a MATLAB file which filters the data organise the data in coordinate order, and then apply them onto a colored contour map with a preset colour map, allowing us to view parameter. The key parameters I looked at were velocity, & CpT. These planes were also used to calculate other key parameters such as mass flow and uniformity explained in project 1. This MATLAB script proved to be extremely vital in design runs allowing to directly compare duct performance at a smaller scale rather than the full car which the ParaView sweeps provided.

Fan Curve Derivation and Staging

Previously mentioned we looked at defining the fan boundary condition but did not go into detail about how that was applied. In Fluent the fan boundary condition zone is defined through the use of a fan curve. A fan curve is static pressure vs the airflow volume which identifies the relationship between how much air the fan is able to push out at varying levels of resistance, generally as the resistance faced increases the airflow the fan is able to push decreases. Summary of Project

Overall the pipeline was able to be completed successfully and with the architecture now built in PyFluent and on GitHub it is easy to use for any upcoming design runs, along with unit tests to test any changes before being merged into the main branch.
